Duck Soup

The mirror scene

1933

Rufus T. Firefly (Groucho Marx) returns home to find his "reflection" behaving oddly — because there is no mirror, only a doorway, and the figure is Pinky (Harpo Marx) in an identical nightshirt, nightcap and greasepaint moustache. What follows is cinema's most perfect mime duet: Groucho tips his hat, Harpo tips his; Groucho dances, Harpo dances, matched beat for beat. Then the symmetry deliberately cracks — impossible spins, dropped poses — and Chico Marx strolls in wearing the same disguise, tripling the chaos as all three shuffle positions to bamboozle Groucho.

Why it works Harpo's stone-faced precision makes the impossible feel logical, and each broken reflection tops the last.

Impact the most imitated gag in film history, from I Love Lucy to Bugs Bunny. Note: the mirror gag predates the film (vaudeville tradition; Max Linder filmed one in 1921) — this is the definitive version. Release-era response is undocumented; today, its timing still plays.

Airplane!

"Don't call me Shirley"

1980

With both pilots incapacitated by bad fish, traumatized ex-pilot Ted Striker (Robert Hays) must land the plane. Dr. Rumack (Leslie Nielsen), grave as a surgeon, asks: "Can you fly this plane and land it?" Ted: "Surely you can't be serious." Rumack, without a flicker: "I am serious… and don't call me Shirley." The brilliance is structural: Airplane! plays a disaster movie dead straight, so Nielsen — a serious actor — delivers idiotic wordplay with total conviction while the stakes are life and death. The line lands because nobody on screen knows it's a joke.

Impact AFI-listed among the great movie quotes; it made Nielsen, at 54, the defining deadpan comedian of his generation and the template for the entire ZAZ style. How the laughs were ranked

I ranked on funniness × comedic craft × lasting cultural footprint: each entry had to work as a self-contained set piece, demonstrate a distinct comic mechanism (verbal, physical, situational, absurdist — no single style dominates), and leave a mark through quotation, imitation, or influence on filmmakers.

I spread picks across nine decades and four continents, capped every franchise at its single best scene, credited originals over remakes, and let recency count against longevity — a 2023 scene cannot outrank a 1933 one on footprint alone. Where dates or authorship are disputed, it’s noted inline.
